Reflective Summary

My six-week teaching practicum at Gonigoda Sri Piyarathana Central College, Katugastota, Kandy, was one of the most important experiences of my undergraduate journey. Although I had studied theories, teaching methods and lesson planning for nearly three and a half years, entering a real classroom as a teacher was completely different. I completed 45 hours of training from 7 September to 16 October 2026, mainly teaching Grades 7A, 7B, 8A and 8B, while also taking relief periods in Grades 9, 10 and 11. Before beginning the internship, I was excited, but more than anything, I was nervous. For the first time, I was responsible not only for teaching English but also for managing a real classroom with learners from different backgrounds, abilities, personalities and levels of motivation.

On my first day, I did not try to do too much. I observed the school environment, watched how the students behaved, spoke with several class teachers and tried to understand the context in which I would be teaching. Even after this, my first few lessons were challenging. Some students talked continuously, some were easily distracted, and some did not take me seriously because I was a new student-teacher. Grade 8B was particularly difficult to manage at the beginning. Initially, I believed that being very strict was the best way to maintain discipline. My strong voice projection helped me gain their attention, but as the weeks passed, I understood that classroom management could not depend only on a loud voice or strictness. Students responded much better when I guided them, listened to them, supported them and gave them a reason to participate.

One of the main difficulties I noticed was that many students were afraid to speak English. Even when they knew the answer, they sometimes hesitated to say it in front of the class. Therefore, I gradually increased pair work, group work and short speaking activities so that learners did not always have to speak alone in front of everyone. For example, during the Past Continuous lesson, learners participated in a miming game in which they guessed actions and produced sentences. In the Prepositions lesson, the “Human GPS” activity allowed one learner to give directions while the partner followed the route, and then they changed roles. I also used short role plays, question-and-answer activities and teacher-controlled Wordwall activities. These activities were especially useful because they gave learners a meaningful reason to use English instead of simply repeating sentences after the teacher. This reflected my growing understanding of Communicative Language Teaching, where learners need opportunities to actually use the language for a purpose.

Technology was another effective part of my teaching. I was fortunate to have access to a Smart Board, and I regularly used PowerPoint presentations, Wordwall activities and visual materials. I also used my tablet when lessons included unfamiliar places, objects or situations. Instead of only describing them, I showed students pictures so they could clearly understand what was being discussed. Their reactions showed me how valuable visual support could be. They became excited, asked questions and were much more interested in the passages when they could connect the words with real images. However, I also learned that technology itself does not make a lesson successful. It worked best when it supported interaction and understanding rather than becoming the main focus of the lesson.

One of my favourite lessons was the Grade 8 lesson on singular and plural nouns. Instead of immediately giving all the rules, students first looked at examples and tried to identify the changes themselves. The “Throw the Pencil Case” activity created a lively classroom atmosphere because different students had the opportunity to select words and write the plural forms on the Smart Board. Later, they completed an individual activity and checked their answers with peers. Guided discovery became increasingly important in my teaching because it allowed students to think before I explained the answer. Thornbury (1999) explains the value of allowing learners to notice grammatical patterns, and I experienced this practically during the internship. Students were more interested when they felt that they had discovered something themselves.

The most successful strategy that I developed independently during the practicum was my “mini-teacher” system. I selected five students who usually obtained the highest marks and divided the remaining students among them. Each of these five students became responsible for supporting a small group of classmates. The results were much better than I expected. Learners who usually needed more support were able to ask a classmate without feeling embarrassed, while fast finishers no longer had to sit and wait after completing their work. Instead, they helped others. Eventually, even weaker learners began completing activities, and I noticed greater cooperation and unity within the class. This experience strengthened my belief in peer learning. Johnson and Johnson (1999) emphasize the importance of cooperation and individual responsibility in learning, and I could see these ideas working naturally in my classroom.

“Teaching is not simply delivering the knowledge that I have.”

The mixed-ability nature of the classes also taught me the importance of differentiation. Some learners completed tasks very quickly, so I kept additional worksheets ready for early finishers. At the same time, some learners required much more support. I prepared simpler visual or colouring-based activities for particular learners who would otherwise have been left without meaningful participation. This taught me that treating every student exactly the same does not always mean teaching fairly. Learners may need different forms of support to remain included in the same classroom. Vygotsky’s (1978) concept of scaffolding also became meaningful to me during these moments because I realised that the support given by the teacher or peers should help learners do something they could not yet do completely independently.

Not every lesson went according to plan. Sometimes an activity took longer than expected, students became overexcited during games, or I included too much content for one period. Time management was therefore one of the areas I had to improve. My teacher-in-charge and supervisor also encouraged me to balance teacher talking time and student talking time. In the beginning, I sometimes explained too much because I wanted to make sure that everyone understood. Later, I tried to give shorter explanations and create more opportunities for students to discover, discuss, practise and correct their own work. Peer checking and individual exit responses also became more common in my later lessons. Black and Wiliam (1998) highlight the importance of gathering evidence of individual learning, and this helped me understand that a whole class shouting the correct answer does not necessarily mean that every learner has understood.

My experience was not limited to the lessons in my fixed timetable. During relief periods in Grades 10 and 11, I worked with examination questions, past papers and the government-provided I Can and I Will workbook. In Grade 9, I usually continued the lesson they had recently been studying. For unexpected relief periods, I kept flexible language activities ready, including Word Clusters, Running Dictation, Back to the Board and Radio Dictation. These experiences taught me to be prepared and adaptable because a teacher cannot always predict what will happen during a school day.

I also became involved in school life beyond classroom teaching. I participated in weekly assemblies and worked as an assistant teacher for Grade 7B when their class teacher was away for paper marking. I helped the students prepare their morning assembly, including announcements, speeches and news reading. Another memorable experience was helping to organise the Children’s Day celebration by coordinating with the Leo Club of Kelaniya and the Leo Club of the University of Kelaniya. I was grateful that they accepted my request and supported the children by providing treats and conducting an after-school job-pathways workshop. These experiences helped me understand that the responsibilities of a teacher extend far beyond completing textbook lessons.

Looking back at the six weeks, the greatest change was not simply in my lesson plans but in myself. I entered the school believing that successful classroom management mainly required strict control. I left understanding that learners respond more positively when a teacher is firm when necessary but also friendly, patient, supportive and willing to understand them. I learned to observe learners more carefully, change an activity when it was not working, provide different kinds of support, use technology purposefully and give students more responsibility for their own learning. Most importantly, I learned that teaching is not simply delivering the knowledge that I have. It is about creating an environment where students feel confident enough to participate, make mistakes, help one another and continue learning. My practicum did not make me a perfect teacher, but it helped me become a more reflective, flexible and learner-centred one, and it strengthened my desire to continue developing as an English language teacher.
